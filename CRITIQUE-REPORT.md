## The four questions, answered directly

### 1. Does the trust model survive an adversarial reading?

**Partly. The trust argument survives. The privacy argument does not, in the shipped
configuration, and the README sells the privacy argument first.**

The bilateral claim is genuinely load-bearing *for trust*. "Bob's contract cannot see
Ethereum even though Bob can" is a real and non-obvious distinction, and it does collapse
the third-party assumption to nothing. That part is honest and it is well argued.

It is not load-bearing for privacy, and here is the mechanical reason: to register an
attestation Bob's watcher must compute
`leaf_hash(lock_id, amount, counterparty, expiry, salt)`. **You cannot compute that leaf
without the amount.** Bob also generates the salt and holds the lock id, so he can
recompute `nullifier_of(lock_id, salt)` and link the on-chain nullifier to the exact
attestation. In the bilateral deployment the counterparty knows the amount, knows which
lock it was, and can deanonymise the proof.

Now put that next to the pitch. README line 20 and PRD Scene 2 both say: *"Revealing the
amount tells your counterparty exactly how much room you have. That is not a privacy
nicety, it is your negotiating position."* That is the emotional core of the whole
narrative, and **the shipped trust model contradicts it.** They cannot both be true in
one deployment. A judge who reads the README top to bottom — which the README explicitly
invites, "Read this part, it is the honest centre of the design" — walks straight into it
about 100 lines apart.

The README is roughly 80% honest already: it does say "privacy from everyone who is not
Bob". But that clause is doing enormous work in a subordinate position, and it is
contradicted by the framing above it rather than reconciling with it.

**Does the ZK become redundant? No, but the honest answer is narrower than the one being
sold — and narrower than this report's own first attempt at it.** In the bilateral case
the ZK buys **exactly one thing: privacy from the chain and from every third party.** The
settlement is on-ledger; the terms are not. That is real, and it is not "hide your
position from your counterparty".

The tempting second item — a machine-checkable gate, so Bob's *contract* can release
without Bob in the loop — is real value but **it is not bought by the zero-knowledge
layer.** A registry of plaintext `(lock_id, amount, counterparty)` tuples plus a
membership check hands the contract the identical on-chain predicate with no proof system
anywhere. The gate is bought by the attestation registry. This report claimed two items
in its first pass and a parallel adversarial read was right to subtract one; see
*Concurrency* below.

**The rescue, which was not in any document and is now the strongest line in the trust
section:** `prove_funds_in_flight` does not change when the trust model changes. The
circuit proves membership in a tree and never learns which attestor inserted the leaf.
Going from one attestor to k-of-n to bonded attestors to a light client is a change to
`register_attestation` alone. That converts the ladder from a wish-list into an
architectural property a judge can verify by reading 40 lines of Compact — and it is what
makes "privacy from the counterparty is rung two" a roadmap rather than an excuse.

Applied as E-4. Thesis field 5 already demanded the trust assumption be "stated, never
hidden"; it was stated but not reconciled, and the gap between those is where this would
have been found by someone else.

### 3. Does the demo script make a judge WITNESS the privacy property?

**No. It makes them look at a JSON object and take the developer's word for what is not
in it. This was the single worst finding and it is now fixed in the script.**

The mechanism, precisely. `frontend/src/lib/demo.ts` `readLedger()` returns a
hand-authored five-field object — `attestor_registered`, `attestor_id`, `merkle_root`,
`nullifiers`, `fills` — with every value truncated by `.slice(0, 24) + '…'`. PRD Section
4.5 and ARCHITECTURE Section 9 both described that pane as "a literal dump of public
ledger state". It is not. It is a projection the developer chose the keys of.

Why that is fatal rather than cosmetic: **the right pane would look exactly the same if
the contract DID write the amount and the renderer simply had no `amount` key.** There is
nothing on screen that could distinguish those two worlds. Scene 4's stage direction —
"scroll the ledger dump so the judge sees the absence" — is asking the judge to trust the
renderer, which is the one thing this product says they should never have to do. Absence
is unwitnessable.

The same defect is in the test suite. `never exposes the locked amount` constructs a
four-field object and asserts `expect(dump).not.toContain('50000')`. No field in that
object is ever a decimal amount, so the assertion cannot fail. It proves nothing. It also
contains `root: Array.from(sim.public.attestations.root().field ?? [])` — the `?? []`
silently passes the test if `.field` is ever undefined. And the fourth "privacy" test
asserts `fills === 1n`, which is a duplicate of the money-path test and is not a privacy
assertion at all. So "four of the tests mechanically assert the privacy property" (PRD
Section 1) was an overclaim: about one and a half do real work.

**The fix is to replace absence with indistinguishability**, because indistinguishability
is a contrast and a contrast can be seen. Same lock id, same salt, same counterparty,
same expiry, same threshold, two amounts a hundred times apart. Both prove. Then diff the
two public ledger states:

| Field | Relation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `attestor`, `attestor_registered`, `fills` | identical | unaffected by amount |
| `spent` (nullifier set) | **byte-identical** | `nullifier_of(lock_id, salt)` does not depend on the amount |
| `attestations.root()` | differs | commits to the leaf under a 256-bit secret salt |

Every field an observer can read and interpret is identical across a 100x difference, and
the one field that changes is a hash that changes exactly as much for a one-unit
difference. **That is a demonstration. "The amount is not printed here" is a claim.** It
is also the line the video needs: *"you are not looking at an amount we chose not to
print, you are looking at two completely different amounts the chain cannot tell apart."*

Scene 4 rewritten (E-1). The test is specified in PRD Section 4.2a and scheduled as PLAN
Task 2b.1. Making the pane itself honest is E-3, deferred to build because it changes
existing frontend behaviour.

### 4. Does the positioning drift into the saturated clusters?

**Yes, and worse than a drift: the closing twenty seconds of the demo video filed Aval
into both clusters by name.**

Scene 6's voiceover was: *"The primitive is the same for proving KYC passed, proving
reserves cover the book, proving an invoice is real."* The project's own intel Kill List
names "Credit scores, solvency proofs, proof-of-reserves (**15 competitors**)" and
"Identity, KYC, credential disclosure (**8 competitors**)" as the two killed categories.
So the last thing a judge hears, after two minutes and forty seconds spent escaping those
clusters, is the project reading its own kill list aloud. It also trips Thesis field 6's
drift tripwires — "a ZK credit score", "an identity wallet" — verbatim. The README roadmap
table did the same in writing: `kyc_passed AND jurisdiction NOT IN sanctioned` and
`reserves >= liabilities`.

The blind peer cross-review found this independently and went further. Given only
`research/research-brief.md` and `PRD.md`, and told to discount the PRD's self-assessment,
it returned **similar**, not differentiated, with this reasoning: the construction —
attestor registers a Merkle leaf, holder proves a private-value predicate disclosing only
a boolean, nullifier prevents reuse — is structurally what the solvency cluster and the
identity cluster are both building. Different vertical label, same circuit shape. A judge
who has just read fifteen proof-of-reserves circuits will open `inflight.compact` and
recognise it.

**I accept that verdict over my own.** Category emptiness (0 of 59) is a label claim, and
label claims do not survive someone reading the source. Differentiation has to be
mechanical.

The mechanical differentiation does exist, it was just never argued. Three things in this
contract are settlement-specific and are absent from every solvency and identity entry
because those entries do not need them:

- **Single use.** The nullifier is derived from the private `lock_id` and `salt`, so one
  lock backs exactly one proof, ever. A reserves claim is a *reusable* statement about a
  standing balance; reuse is the normal case, not an attack.
- **Counterparty bound.** `counterparty` is hashed into the leaf, so an attestation issued
  for Bob is worthless to Carol. A credential is a broadcast claim, deliberately
  presentable to anyone who asks.
- **Expiring on ledger block time.** `kernel.blockTimeLessThan`, not a caller-supplied
  timestamp. A balance proof is about now; an in-flight proof is about a window that
  closes.

Nine of the 22 tests cover those three. Together they make the output a **single-use,
counterparty-bound, expiring bearer instrument** — the difference between a proof you
*show* and a proof you *spend*. That sentence now appears in the PRD, the README and the
Scene 6 rewrite, and Scene 6 closes on trade-finance settlement moments
(delivery-versus-payment, letters of credit, invoice factoring) which have zero
competitors in the field and reinforce the product name instead of the clusters.

**Assessment: `similar`** — reconciled down from my initial `differentiated` after the

This is the assessment of the documents **as critique found them**. With E-5 applied (the

### Overlap zones

| Zone | Teams likely building it | Our differentiator |
|---|---|---|
| Attested-commitment + ZK threshold predicate + nullifier | ~23 of 59 (15 credit/solvency, 8 identity) | Single-use, counterparty-bound, expiring. Three mechanisms, nine tests. Not the construction — what the construction is made into. |
| "Prove a threshold is cleared without revealing the balance" | the entire 15-entry solvency cluster | The fact is an *event* (a lock: monotone, irrevocable, uniquely identified, time-bounded) not a *balance* (mutable, standing, re-assertable) |
| Merkle-membership privacy on Midnight | unknown, likely several | `HistoricMerkleTree` specifically, so a proof survives concurrent insertions; and disclosing the computed root rather than the leaf, which hides *which* attestation was used |

### Differentiation strengths

- **The vertical is genuinely empty.** 0 of 59 on in-flight/pending settlement state, and it maps to Midnight's own published 2026 language ("institutional execution").
- **The contract is small and every line is defensible.** Six annotated `disclose()` sites, each stating what an observer actually learns. That is a 40%-Engineering artifact.
- **`pureCircuits.leaf_hash` is reused off-chain by the watcher.** One hash definition system-wide, enforced by the compiler rather than by discipline. Most entries will reimplement their hash in TypeScript and not notice the divergence.
- **Zero credentials, no Docker, no proof server.** A judge verifies offline in under a minute. This is also a direct answer to the loudest documented complaint in Midnight's own docs issues, which a prior edition scored as a criterion.
- **`kernel.blockTimeLessThan` rather than a caller-supplied timestamp.** Small, correct, and the kind of thing a Midnight engineer notices.

### Differentiation gaps

- The construction reads familiar until the instrument argument is made. Fixed in prose; must survive into the deck and video.
- No deployment anywhere on Midnight. Justified by the rules and stated plainly, but it means the entire Midnight integration is compile + simulate. A competitor with a live testnet contract will *look* deeper even if they are not.
- The demo's credibility rests on a pane whose honesty the judge cannot check. E-3.

## Sponsor Integration Depth

Midnight is not a sponsor alongside others, it is the platform, so this is the 40%

| Sponsor | Surface used | Depth | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Midnight / Compact | `HistoricMerkleTree`, `Set`, `Counter`, `Boolean`, `Bytes<32>`, `Uint<64>`, `persistentHash`, `merkleTreePathRoot`, `MerkleTreePath`, `kernel.blockTimeLessThan`, `disclose` information-flow discipline, witness declarations, exported `pure circuit` reused off-chain via `pureCircuits`, `compact-runtime` `CircuitContext` simulation with injected block time, generated-ledger `findPathForLeaf` | **deep** | deep-narrow: no deployment, no wallet integration, no proof-server path, three circuits totalling 157 lines |

Against the rubric's own words for Engineering — "Compact contract compiles, private state
management, dual-ledger understanding, organized repo, clear README" — all five are hit,
and the dual-ledger understanding is hit unusually well. `HistoricMerkleTree` over a
`Set` of leaves (because a Set check forces disclosing the leaf and destroys
unlinkability) is a real design decision with a real reason, and it is the kind of thing
that separates someone who read the docs from someone who fought the compiler.

The honest caveat is breadth. A judge comparing against an entry with five contracts and
a testnet deploy may read 157 lines as small. The counter-argument — depth over breadth,
every disclose annotated, information-flow-enforced — is made in the README but never
quantified, and I would not spend the remaining hours trying to quantify it. The
instrument argument (E-5) is a better use of the same slot because it makes the smallness
look deliberate rather than incomplete.

Two things the contract does not do that are worth one line each in "What is NOT built",
because a judge with security instincts will spot both and it is better to name them
first: `register_attestor` asserts `!attestor_registered` with no rotation or revocation
path, so a lost key permanently bricks the registry; and the nullifier set is
per-deployment, so cross-counterparty reuse is prevented by off-chain watcher policy
rather than by a contract invariant. Both applied under E-4.
